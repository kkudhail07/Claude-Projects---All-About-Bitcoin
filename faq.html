Common questions

Your questions, answered

The doubts and worries almost everyone has when they first look at Bitcoin — answered honestly and in plain English. Tap a question to open it.

Is it too late to get into Bitcoin?

Nobody knows the future, and Bitcoin may go up or down. But it's still young and small compared to assets like gold, and you don't need to buy a whole coin — you can start with a few pounds. The more useful question than "is it too late?" is "do I understand it?" Learn first, then decide.

Isn't Bitcoin just a scam or a Ponzi scheme?

Bitcoin itself isn't a company or a scheme — it's open software anyone can inspect, with no boss collecting your money and no promise of returns. That said, the space around it is full of scams that use Bitcoin's name. Bitcoin the network is honest; some people in the industry are not. Our safety guide covers how to spot the difference.

Is Bitcoin bad for the environment?

Bitcoin does use energy — that's part of what keeps it secure. But a growing share comes from renewables and otherwise-wasted energy (like flared gas or surplus hydro), and miners often set up where power would otherwise go unused. It's a genuine debate worth reading both sides of, not a settled "yes" or "no".

Isn't Bitcoin only used by criminals?

Every form of money — cash most of all — gets used by criminals. The difference is that Bitcoin's ledger is public and permanent, so illicit use is actually easier to trace than physical cash. Study after study finds illegal activity is a small and shrinking slice of Bitcoin use.

Why is the price so up and down?

Bitcoin is still small and new, so relatively little buying or selling moves the price a lot — and it trades 24/7 worldwide on emotion as much as fundamentals. Volatility is the price of being early. It tends to calm down as something grows, but for now, only commit what you can comfortably hold through big swings.

Can governments just ban it?

A government can make it harder to use locally — restricting exchanges, for example — and some have tried. But Bitcoin runs on computers all over the world with no central office to shut down, so no single country can switch it off. Several nations have actually moved the other way, embracing it.

What happens if I lose my phone or password?

If you hold your own bitcoin, what really matters is your recovery phrase — a list of words that can restore your wallet on a new device. Lose your phone but keep that phrase safe, and you're fine. Lose the phrase with no backup, and the coins are gone for good. That's why backing it up carefully is step one — see staying safe.

Is Bitcoin anonymous?

Not really — it's more like a pseudonym. Every transaction is recorded on a public ledger forever. Your name isn't attached directly, but addresses can often be linked back to people, especially once you've used an exchange that knows your identity. Think "public and traceable," not "secret."

Do I have to buy a whole bitcoin?

No. Each bitcoin divides into 100 million tiny units called satoshis, or "sats." You can buy a few pounds' worth and own a slice — most people start small.

Isn't Bitcoin "backed by nothing"?

Modern national currencies aren't backed by gold either — they're backed by trust and law. Bitcoin is backed by its rules: a fixed supply, an open network, and the enormous amount of energy and computing that secures it. Whether that's "enough" is exactly what these books and videos help you decide.
